STORAGE

Before you delete an app, you should know which storage it uses and what Kubernetes will do with that storage afterward. By the end of this guide, you will be able to find the Persistent Volumes behind an app, read their reclaim policy and status, and spot disks that are still running with nothing attached. This matters because cloud disks keep billing you even when no pod is using them.

Why this matters

A Deployment creates pods, and pods are disposable. Storage is handled by two separate objects. A Persistent Volume Claim (PVC) is the app's request for disk space. A Persistent Volume (PV) is the actual piece of storage, which usually maps to a real disk in your cloud account. Deleting the Deployment removes the pods, but it does not automatically remove the PVC or the PV. Each object has its own lifecycle, so you have to check each one.

Step 1: Find the storage your app uses

Start by listing the PVCs in the namespace where the app runs. The namespace is demo in this example. The claim is the link between your app and the disk, so it tells you what is at stake before you delete anything.

kubectl get pvc -n demo

Expected output

NAME       STATUS   VOLUME                                     CAPACITY   ACCESS MODES   STORAGECLASS   AGE
web-data   Bound    pvc-3f9c2a1e-7b4d-4e8a-9c21-5d6e0f1a2b3c   5Gi        RWO            standard       12d

The VOLUME column gives you the name of the PV bound to this claim. Write it down. You will need it in later steps.

Step 2: Check the reclaim policy on that volume

Next, look at the PV itself. The reclaim policy decides what happens to the disk when its claim is deleted. Delete removes the disk along with the claim. Retain keeps the disk and its data, and leaves cleanup to you.

kubectl get pv pvc-3f9c2a1e-7b4d-4e8a-9c21-5d6e0f1a2b3c

Expected output

NAME                                       CAPACITY   ACCESS MODES   RECLAIM POLICY   STATUS   CLAIM             STORAGECLASS   REASON   AGE
pvc-3f9c2a1e-7b4d-4e8a-9c21-5d6e0f1a2b3c   5Gi        RWO            Retain           Bound    demo/web-data     standard                12d

Here the policy is Retain. That means deleting the claim will leave the disk behind. If you see Delete instead, the disk will be removed with the claim, which is safer for billing but destroys the data. Either way, you now know what to expect. If you want to confirm the policy at the source, kubectl describe pv shows the same field along with the backing disk details.

Step 3: Delete the app and the claim, then watch the volume

Now remove the Deployment and the PVC. Deleting the Deployment stops the pods. Deleting the PVC releases the claim. Do these in order so you can see the effect of each one.

kubectl delete deployment web -n demo
kubectl delete pvc web-data -n demo

Expected output

deployment.apps "web" deleted
persistentvolumeclaim "web-data" deleted

Then check the volume again.

kubectl get pv

Expected output

NAME                                       CAPACITY   ACCESS MODES   RECLAIM POLICY   STATUS     CLAIM             STORAGECLASS   REASON   AGE
pvc-3f9c2a1e-7b4d-4e8a-9c21-5d6e0f1a2b3c   5Gi        RWO            Retain           Released   demo/web-data     standard                12d

The volume did not disappear. Its status is now Released, which means no claim is bound to it. Because the policy is Retain, the disk still exists in your cloud account and still costs money. The kubectl get pv command lists every PV in the cluster, not just the ones your apps use, so it also shows storage from apps you deleted long ago.

Step 4: Decide what to do with each Released volume

Once you have a list of Released volumes, treat each one as a question, not a deletion. Ask the owner whether the data is still needed. A Retain volume might hold a database backup that someone is keeping on purpose. If the data is not needed, you can switch the policy to Delete and then remove the PV object. Deleting the PV with a Delete policy also removes the cloud disk.

kubectl patch pv pvc-3f9c2a1e-7b4d-4e8a-9c21-5d6e0f1a2b3c -p '{"spec":{"persistentVolumeReclaimPolicy":"Delete"}}'
kubectl delete pv pvc-3f9c2a1e-7b4d-4e8a-9c21-5d6e0f1a2b3c

Expected output

persistentvolume/pvc-3f9c2a1e-7b4d-4e8a-9c21-5d6e0f1a2b3c patched
persistentvolume "pvc-3f9c2a1e-7b4d-4e8a-9c21-5d6e0f1a2b3c" deleted

Run kubectl get pv again afterward. The volume should be gone from the list. Then check your cloud console to confirm the disk was removed. Kubernetes reports the PV deletion, but your cloud account is where the bill comes from, so verify there too.

Common mistakes

  • Deleting a Retain volume without asking. The symptom is that the data is gone and someone asks where it went. The fix is to confirm with the owner before you patch or delete a PV.
  • Assuming the Deployment deleted the storage. The symptom is a cloud bill that keeps growing after the pods are gone. The fix is to run kubectl get pvc -n demo and kubectl get pv after every cleanup.
  • Deleting the PVC while the pod still runs. The symptom is a PVC stuck in Terminating until the pod stops. The fix is to delete the Deployment first, then the claim.
  • Ignoring Released volumes. The symptom is a PV listed as Released for months. The fix is to review kubectl get pv on a schedule and act on each row.

Quick recap

  1. List the claims in the app's namespace with kubectl get pvc -n demo.
  2. Note the bound PV and check its RECLAIM POLICY with kubectl get pv.
  3. Delete the Deployment, then the PVC, and check that the PV shows Released.
  4. For each Released volume, confirm with the owner before changing the policy or deleting the PV.
  5. Verify in your cloud console that the disk is gone.
