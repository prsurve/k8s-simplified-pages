Kubernetes isn't new. AI just made it scary again, and your pods are the first thing to break.

Kubernetes Pods keep restarting and AI made it feel like the end of the world? It's not. Kubernetes has been around since 2015. AI workloads just push it harder, so small YAML mistakes now show up as restart loops. The good news: the fix is usually a number in your config, and kubectl describe pod tells you which one. Takeaway: Kubernetes runs what you declare, not what you meant. Read the events before you blame the platform. Save this for the next time a pod crashes at 2 AM. Which error gets you first, OOMKilled or CrashLoopBackOff? Comment below. — @k8s.simplified · Kubernetes, without the fluff. "Your AI app is down, and the pods keep restarting every few seconds. Kubernetes is not new. It came out in 2015 and has been running banks and streaming apps for years. AI made it scary because big models need big memory and GPUs, and a small mistake now costs real money every restart. Kubernetes runs on one idea. You declare what you want, and it keeps working until reality matches that. Say you declare three replicas. When one pod dies, the controller creates a replacement within seconds, and the count goes back to three. But it only runs what you declared. One wrong memory limit in your YAML creates a restart loop that never ends. Check the last state. If it says OOMKilled with exit code 137, your container ran out of memory. The platform did not break. Read the events, fix the number in your YAML, apply it, then watch the rollout finish before you walk away. Kubernetes runs what you declare, not what you meant. Read the events before you blame the platform."
